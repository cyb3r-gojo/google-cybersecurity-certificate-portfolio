# Parking lot USB drive
This repository documents an investigation into a suspicious USB device found in the parking lot of Rhetorical Hospital. The case study examines the contents of the USB drive, evaluates potential attacker motives, and analyzes the security risks associated with USB baiting attacks

## Scenario
You are part of the security team at Rhetorical Hospital and arrive to work one morning. On the ground of the parking lot, you find a USB stick with the hospital's logo printed on it. There’s no one else around who might have dropped it, so you decide to pick it up out of curiosity.

You bring the USB drive back to your office where the team has virtualization software installed on a workstation. Virtualization software can be used for this very purpose because it’s one of the only ways to safely investigate an unfamiliar USB stick. The  software works by running a simulated instance of the computer on the same workstation. This simulation isn’t connected to other files or networks, so the USB drive can’t affect other systems if it happens to be infected with malicious software.

## Solution

| **Section**          | **Description**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Contents**         | The USB drive contained a mixture of personal and work-related files belonging to Jorge Bailey, including family and pet photos that may reveal personally identifiable information (PII). It also stored sensitive HR documents like a new hire letter and employee shift schedules. Storing personal and work files together increases privacy risks and the possibility of organizational data exposure.                                                                          |
| **Attacker Mindset** | An attacker could use the personal and HR files to profile Jorge, identify relatives, and craft targeted phishing or social engineering attacks. Documents such as shift schedules and onboarding letters could be leveraged to impersonate employees or infiltrate hospital systems. The USB itself might have been intentionally planted to entice someone into plugging it in and triggering malware deployment.                                                                  |
| **Risk Analysis**    | USB baiting attacks may contain malware such as keyloggers, ransomware, or remote-access trojans, which could compromise systems if opened by another employee. Personal and HR information on the drive could enable identity theft, spear-phishing, or targeted attacks. Mitigation strategies include disabling unauthorized USB ports, enforcing endpoint protection, using virtualization for safe investigation, and training employees on proper handling of unknown devices. |

### Conclusion
This case study highlights how a seemingly harmless USB device can pose significant security risks through malware delivery or exposure of sensitive information. Following best practices—such as never opening unknown devices on production machines, enforcing strict access controls, and educating employees—helps protect both individuals and organizations from USB baiting attacks.
